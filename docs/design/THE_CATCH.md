# THE CATCH — why there are rings in orbit, and what they're for

### ⚖ Terry, 2026-07-29: *"the ring lamp chase would be fun but it doesn't make a lot of sense. We need
### some story there and we need the rings to have some functional look and use that maybe a
### spacefaring civilization would have. This game doesn't just need to be fun, it's got to make sense."*

He's right, and the fix is not decoration — it's asking what this civilization would ACTUALLY build,
then discovering that the answer explains every piece of gameplay the space leg already has.

---

## 1 · THE PROBLEM WITH RINGS

Five glowing hoops in a line that you fly through is a **racing game trope**. It has no in-world
reason to exist, so it reads as a tutorial obstacle course wearing a sci-fi hat. Worse, it makes the
whole space leg feel like a minigame bolted onto a world that otherwise takes itself seriously —
a working salvage town with a dockmaster, contracts, and a debt economy.

The test any answer has to pass: **if you deleted the player, would this thing still be here, and
would somebody still be paying to maintain it?**

---

## 2 · THE ANSWER: A MASS DRIVER NEEDS A CATCHER

The Moss is a poor, wet, heavy-industry moon. Its whole economy is **getting mass off the ground** —
ore, scrap, refined sludge — and rockets are the expensive way to do that. The cheap way, for a world
with more electricity than fuel, is a **mass driver**: a long electromagnetic track that flings
sealed cargo pods into orbit with no engine aboard at all.

That solves launch. It creates a new problem: **a pod thrown that way arrives in orbit going far too
fast and has nothing to slow itself with.**

So the orbital side has to catch it. **THE CATCH** is that catcher: a line of **arrestor rings**
strung along the pods' arrival path. Each ring is an electromagnetic brake. A pod passes through the
bore, the coils bleed off a share of its velocity, and it coasts to the next ring slower than it
arrived. Five rings, five stages, and by the last one it's drifting slowly enough for a tug to grab.

It is, almost exactly, **an aircraft carrier's arrestor wires** — or a harbour's approach channel —
built for cargo instead of aircraft.

### Why it's a LINE
Because the pod flies a ballistic arc and can't steer. The rings have to sit **on that arc**, in
order, spaced by how much speed each one can take. The corridor's shape isn't a level designer's
choice — it's the trajectory. *(That is why the course climbs and bends: it's following the arc.)*

### Why it's still lit when nobody uses it
The Moss's industry has been shrinking for a generation. Nobody's thrown a pod up the Catch in
years. But the corridor is still the only volume above the Moss that is **swept** — routinely cleared
of debris — so every pilot in the Roads still flies it, for a reason that has nothing to do with what
it was built for. **Infrastructure outliving its purpose, used for something else, is the most
Ziptide thing in this game.**

## 3 · EVERY GAMEPLAY ELEMENT NOW HAS A REASON

This is the part that matters. Nothing below is new work — it's the same content, finally explained.

| Already built | Used to be | Now is |
|---|---|---|
| **5 rings in a line** | a slalom | five braking stages on a cargo trajectory |
| **Flying through them** | "the objective" | flying **the swept lane** — the only debris-cleared volume; outside it is uncleared junk |
| **The chase lights** | a pretty effect | **sequenced approach lighting** — the same "rabbit" strobe train real runways use, running toward the next gate so guidance (and pilots) can read the corridor's direction |
| **Passed rings going green** | arbitrary | a gate reporting **CLEAR** — you're past it, it has nothing more to take |
| **The drift rocks off-course** | parallax dressing | the **uncleared debris** that explains why the swept lane is worth flying |
| **The salvage drones** | targets | **ring-tenders**: maintenance drones that station-keep, clear micro-debris and challenge unlogged traffic. They wake because you entered the corridor without a transponder log. They aren't hostile — they're *bureaucratic*, which is why they're non-lethal |
| **The wreck field past the last ring** | set dressing | **THE OVERRUN** — where everything that missed the catch ends up. Missed pods, ships that clipped a ring, tugs that ran dry |
| **THE FIND on a wreck out there** | a quest item on a rock | an object in the place where **things that were never supposed to be here accumulate.** The perfect address for something that doesn't scan |
| **The city below** | unrelated | sits at the ground end of the same system — **THE THROW**, the mass driver, is on its horizon |

## 4 · NAMES (harbour + airfield language — the house dialect)

The Moss already speaks in harbour terms: berth, quay, dockmaster, breakwater, sea wall. Orbital
infrastructure keeps that voice, borrowing from airfields where the function is aviation's:

- **THE ROADS** — the orbital volume above the Moss. *(A roadstead is where ships wait outside a
  harbour. It is exactly what this is.)*
- **THE CATCH** — the arrestor corridor itself.
- **CATCH 1 … CATCH 5** — the individual rings, stenciled on the collar.
- **THE OVERRUN** — the junk field past CATCH 5. *(Airfield term for the ground past a runway end.)*
- **THE THROW** — the ground-side mass driver, visible from Toxic City as a lit track climbing the
  far shore. You came down its corridor.
- **RING-TENDERS** — the maintenance drones.

## 5 · WHAT A CATCH RING LOOKS LIKE (the functional spec)

Not a neon hoop. Everything on it earns its place:

1. **Open truss torus**, ~12 m bore. Lattice, not a solid ring — mass is expensive in orbit, and a
   solid ring buys nothing. Grey-brown alloy, micrometeorite-pitted.
2. **Coil blocks** on the inner face — twelve chunky segments, the actual electromagnets. Ribbed
   casings. This is the working part; it should look heavy and serviceable.
3. **Radiator fins** standing off the outer rim, radially. Braking dumps energy as heat and vacuum
   has nowhere to put it — **fins are the honest tell that this thing does work.** Slightly
   discoloured near the roots.
4. **Sequencer lamps** — a ring of small amber fixtures on the inner rim, running the chase. Small.
   The lamps are jewellery on a machine, not the machine.
5. **Numeral collar** — a flat plate with a big stenciled numeral (1–5) plus faded hazard chevrons,
   readable from a long way out.
6. **Service spar** — a short boom with a drone perch and grab rungs. Somebody used to come here.
7. **Wear**: one ring (CATCH 3) has a **blown coil segment** — a gap, scorch, a welded patch plate —
   and **its sequencer is dead.** The corridor is failing, and the player sees that before anyone
   says it.

**Scale cue:** a cargo pod is about 3 m across; the bore is four pods wide. Rungs and the perch are
human-sized. Keep something human-sized on every ring or the scale reads as a toy.

## 8 · WHAT THIS CHANGES IN CODE (v1, procedural, shipping now)

- `BuildRing` builds the **functional** silhouette: truss torus, twelve coil blocks, radiator fins,
  numeral collar, service spar, sequencer lamp ring.
- **The lamps are their own child object** (`Ring_<i>/Lamps`). Previously the chase repainted the
  ENTIRE ring green, which is a large part of why it read as a toy — a machine doesn't change colour,
  its indicator lights do. `RingCourseLightsRuntime` now paints lamps only.
- **CATCH 3 is dead**: blown coil, patch plate, and **no lamps at all**, so the corridor visibly
  degrades without a line of dialogue.
- **THE THROW** stands on Toxic City's horizon — a lit mass-driver track climbing the far shore, so
  the ground and the orbit are one system rather than two levels.

---

## 9 · WHY THIS IS WORTH THE EFFORT

The rings were the one part of Level 1 a player could reasonably ask "why is that there?" about and
get no answer. Now the answer explains the salvage economy, the drones' non-lethality, the debris,
the location of the artifact, and the reason the city sits where it does — and it costs one evening
of geometry. **Every world after this one inherits the rule: if a structure exists, somebody built it
for a reason, and the reason should be visible in its shape.**
